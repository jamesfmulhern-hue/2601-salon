# Our Building, Our Story: Writing the Life of 2601
### A Ten-Session Community Writing Course for the Residents of 2601
**Taught by James F. Mulhern**

---

## An Invitation

Every building is a village. Behind each door on every floor at 2601 is a life — an arrival story, a triumph, a private sorrow, a friendship struck up in the elevator, a joke that is still funny fifteen years later. Most of it is never written down. It lives only in conversation, and then, eventually, it doesn't live anywhere at all.

This course exists to change that.

Over ten sessions, the residents of 2601 will learn to write the personal essay — clearly, honestly, and well — and will use those new skills to build a shared book: a collection of true, first-person essays about what it has meant to live in this particular building, among these particular neighbors, in this particular slice of a life. No experience is required. No one needs to have written anything since a high school assignment. You only need a memory, a willingness to look at it honestly, and ninety minutes a week.

By the end, we will have a manuscript — a real one, ready for the kind of independent publication available to any of us through print-on-demand services such as Amazon KDP. A book with your neighbors' names on the spine, on the shelf, in the lobby, and in your own hands.

---

## Welcome

Welcome to *Our Building, Our Story* — a ten-session course in personal essay writing, built specifically for the residents of 2601 and organized around a single shared subject: this building, and your life within it.

The first four sessions teach the craft fundamentals every essayist needs — brainstorming, structuring an essay, drafting a scene, and revising with a clear eye — using 2601 itself as the shared material for every exercise. The next five sessions each introduce one essay type particular to a residential community: the arrival story, the neighbor portrait, the happy memory, the hard memory, the funniest memory, and the most unforgettable moment. The final session is a workshop and manuscript-planning meeting, where the group decides, together, how the individual essays will become one collective book.

This is not a memoir-writing marathon. Each essay is short — the kind of piece a person can draft in one sitting and refine in two or three more. The goal is a body of work every contributor can be proud of, gathered into something permanent.

I am glad to be teaching this course, and I am glad you are here.

## What This Course Is

This is a beginner-friendly workshop in the personal essay, using the shared experience of living at 2601 as its subject matter throughout. Each week we will:

- Learn one specific craft skill (brainstorming, outlining, scene-writing, revision, or a particular essay form)
- See that skill demonstrated with short published examples
- Practice the skill directly on 2601 material, in class, by hand or by laptop
- Leave with a clear, short take-home assignment: a complete draft of that week's essay

By Session 10, every participant will have a folder of five to seven short personal essays, each between 500 and 1,200 words, all set at or shaped by 2601 — ready to be assembled, edited, and published as a shared building anthology.

---

## What This Course Is Not

- It is not a gossip session. We write about our own experience of people and events — not to expose, wound, or settle scores.
- It is not a legal risk. Session 4 is devoted entirely to writing about real people and real events without inviting a lawsuit — a subject every contributor must take seriously before anyone's essay is considered for the shared book.
- It is not mandatory disclosure. You choose what to write about. No one will ask you to reveal more than you want to.
- It is not a contest. There will be no ranking of essays. Every finished, honest piece belongs in the anthology if its author wants it there.
- It is not a one-way lecture. Bring your stories. The best material in this course will come from you, not from me.

### Session 1 — Welcome & Brainstorming: Finding Your 2601 Material

### Main Points of the Lesson

1. **Everyone in this room already has enough material for ten essays.** The problem beginning writers face is almost never a shortage of experience — it is not yet knowing how to find it, sort it, and recognize which memories are actually alive. Brainstorming is the tool that solves this.
2. **Brainstorming works by lowering the bar, not raising it.** The goal in the first pass is quantity, not quality. Write down every memory of the building that surfaces, no matter how small, boring, or unfinished it seems. Sorting happens later.
3. **Specific beats general every time.** "I've lived here a long time and made good friends" is not yet an essay. "The night the power went out on the ninth floor and Mrs. Alvarez knocked on every door with a flashlight and a deck of cards" is the beginning of one. Specificity is the whole craft in miniature.
4. **A good brainstorming list has categories.** For this course, useful categories include: arrivals and departures, neighbors, doormen and staff, holidays and building events, small emergencies, the view, the elevator, the lobby, noise, weather, pets, children growing up in the building, losses, celebrations, and anything that made you laugh.
5. **The senses unlock memory faster than thinking does.** Before you try to remember what happened, try to remember what you smelled, heard, or touched. The smell of a hallway, the sound of a particular door, the temperature of the lobby in winter — these sensory details are often the door back into a fuller memory.

### In-Class Practice

*Fifteen minutes.* On a blank page, write "2601" at the top and then, without stopping to judge or polish, list every memory, image, or person connected to the building that comes to mind. Aim for at least twenty items — some can be a single word ("elevator," "Mrs. Alvarez," "moving day," "the blackout"). Do not write full sentences yet. Just make the list as long as possible.

### Discussion Questions

1. Look at your list. Which three items make you feel something the moment you write them down — a small jolt of feeling, whether warm or uncomfortable? Those are usually the strongest material.
2. Is there an item on your list you're surprised you remembered? Why do you think it surfaced?
3. What is the very first memory you have of 2601 — the earliest one, from your first days here? Where does it rank on your list?
4. Which category (arrivals, neighbors, building events, small emergencies, and so on) produced the most items for you? What does that tell you about what you notice and remember?
5. Is there anything on your list you are hesitant to write about? You do not have to explain why — just notice it. We will return to that question in Session 4.

### Homework

Circle the five strongest items on your brainstorm list. For each one, write one sentence that is as specific and concrete as you can make it — not "a funny thing happened with my neighbor," but "Mr. Petrosky once brought his goldfish to the lobby in a soup pot during a fire drill." Bring all five sentences to Session 2.

### Session 2 — Outlining & Planning: Giving a True Story a Shape

### Main Points of the Lesson

1. **A true story is not yet an essay until it has a shape.** Life does not arrive in essay form. The writer's job is to select a beginning, middle, and end from the much longer, messier reality of what actually happened — and that selection is an act of design, not falsification.
2. **The simplest reliable shape is: situation, turn, insight.** Begin with where things stood. Introduce a turn — something changed, arrived, was said, or was lost. End with what you now understand that you did not understand before it happened. This three-part shape underlies almost every successful short personal essay.
3. **An outline is a promise you make to your reader, then keep.** Before drafting, jot four or five lines describing what happens in order. This is not a straitjacket — you can and should deviate once you start writing — but it prevents the wandering, directionless draft that beginning writers most often produce.
4. **Choose your ending before you choose your opening.** Knowing where an essay is going to land — what realization or image will close it — makes every earlier decision easier. Many writers draft the last line or last image first, then build backward toward it.
5. **Cut everything that does not serve the shape you have chosen.** An outline also tells you what to leave out. If a memory does not move the reader from the situation to the insight, it belongs in a different essay, not this one.

### In-Class Practice

*Fifteen minutes.* Choose one of your five sentences from last week's homework. On a fresh page, write four lines: (1) the situation — what things were like before; (2) the turn — what happened, arrived, or changed; (3) one detail you will use to make the turn vivid; (4) the insight — what you now understand that you didn't before. This four-line outline is the skeleton of your Session 3 draft.

### Discussion Questions

1. Try the situation/turn/insight shape on a memory from your list. Does it fit naturally, or does the memory resist that shape? What does that tell you?
2. Think of a favorite story someone has told you about 2601 — maybe at a building party, in the elevator, in passing. Can you identify its shape? Where is the turn?
3. Why might choosing your ending first make the rest of the essay easier to write?
4. Is there a memory on your list that feels important but that you cannot yet find a shape for? Sit with that question rather than solving it today.
5. What is the risk of an essay with no clear shape? What happens to a reader — a neighbor reading your essay in the finished book — when a piece wanders without direction?

### Homework

Write a complete four-line outline (situation, turn, detail, insight) for two more items from your brainstorm list, in addition to the one you outlined in class. Bring all three outlines to Session 3 — you will draft one of them into a full essay.

### Session 3 — Scene, Voice, and the First Draft: Putting the Reader in the Room

### Main Points of the Lesson

1. **Scene is the essay's engine.** A scene puts the reader inside a specific moment — this hallway, this conversation, this hour — with enough sensory and physical detail that they feel present. Most memorable personal essays are built from one or two well-rendered scenes, not from a general summary of events.
2. **Show the moment before you explain it.** Beginning writers often explain a feeling ("I was very moved by my neighbor's kindness") instead of rendering the moment that produced the feeling (what she said, what she was wearing, what the hallway smelled like, how long the silence lasted before either of you spoke). Trust the scene to deliver the feeling without your having to name it.
3. **Voice is simply you, on the page, without pretending to be someone else.** Do not reach for fancy vocabulary or a "writerly" tone. The most effective personal essays sound like a specific, particular person talking honestly — your syntax, your humor, your way of noticing things.
4. **A first draft's only job is to exist.** Many writers stall because they try to make the first sentence perfect before writing the second. Give yourself permission to write badly and quickly. Revision — which we cover later — is where quality is built. Session 3's draft is not meant to be finished.
5. **Dialogue, even remembered imperfectly, brings a scene to life.** You will rarely recall a conversation word for word, and that's fine — reconstruct the gist and the tone as faithfully as you can. A few lines of remembered dialogue almost always outperform a paragraph describing what was discussed.

### In-Class Practice

*Twenty-five minutes.* Take one of your three outlines from Session 2 and draft it as a full scene of 400 to 600 words. Do not worry about the opening line, the ending, or grammar. Get the reader into the room: what did you see, hear, smell; who spoke, and what did they say; what happened, in order, moment by moment. Write straight through without stopping to revise.

### Discussion Questions

1. Read back the scene you just drafted. Where did you slip into explaining a feeling instead of showing the moment that created it? Circle those spots — you will return to them in revision.
2. Is there a line of dialogue in your draft — remembered, reconstructed, or approximate — that carries real weight? What makes it work?
3. What does "voice" mean to you, in your own writing, as opposed to someone else's? Can you point to a sentence in your draft that sounds unmistakably like you?
4. Did permission to write a "bad" first draft change how the writing felt? Was it easier or harder than you expected?
5. What is one physical or sensory detail from your scene that you are especially glad you included?

### Homework

Finish the draft you began in class if you did not complete it. Read it aloud to yourself once, all the way through, before Session 4. Do not revise yet — just notice what you hear.

### Session 4 — Writing About Real People Without a Lawsuit: The Ethics and Law of the Personal Essay

### Main Points of the Lesson

1. **Every essay in this collection will mention real, identifiable neighbors — and that carries legal and ethical weight.** Writing about your own experience is always protected; writing about someone else's private life, or making false factual claims about them, is a different matter. This session exists so that every contributor understands the difference before a single essay goes into the shared manuscript.
2. **Defamation requires a false statement of fact that damages someone's reputation.** Opinions, honestly held and clearly framed as opinions ("I always found him difficult" rather than "he is a criminal"), are on much safer ground than factual assertions you cannot support. If you would not be comfortable saying a specific claim to that person's face, with evidence in hand, do not put it in the essay.
3. **Truth is the essayist's best legal protection — but truth alone is not always enough.** A true but highly private fact about someone (a health condition, a family secret, a financial detail) can still expose a writer to a claim of "invasion of privacy" or "public disclosure of private facts" in some jurisdictions, even when the fact is accurate. When in doubt, ask: is this detail necessary to my story, or am I including it because it is dramatic?
4. **The safest and most respectful practice is to write your own experience of a person, not a verdict on their character.** "I remember feeling shut out when Mr. Kim stopped saying hello in the elevator" is your own honest, defensible experience. "Mr. Kim is a rude, hostile man" is a judgment that adds risk without adding truth.
5. **Practical safeguards, in order of strength:** (1) change identifying names and details for anyone portrayed unflatteringly, unless they have given clear permission to be named; (2) when in doubt, ask the person directly before publication — a short, warm conversation solves most problems; (3) focus on your own interior experience rather than claims about others' motives, which you cannot actually know; (4) avoid including anyone's specific address, unit number, medical information, legal history, or family conflict without their express written consent; (5) when an essay is about a difficult relationship, let complexity and your own uncertainty show on the page — a portrait with some tenderness in it is both truer and legally safer than a one-sided grievance.

### In-Class Practice

*Fifteen minutes.* Take the draft from Session 3. Go through it line by line and mark, in the margin, every sentence that makes a factual claim about another named person (not about yourself). For each one, ask: is this my own experience, clearly framed as such, or is it a claim about their character, motive, or private life? Revise at least one flagged sentence into the safer, more defensible form.

### Discussion Questions

1. Why might a true statement still create legal risk? Can you think of a private fact about a neighbor that, even if accurate, would not belong in a published essay?
2. What is the difference between writing "I felt hurt when she didn't invite me" and "She is a cold, unkind person"? Which one is actually more interesting to read? Why?
3. Is there a person in your Session 3 draft whose name you should consider changing, or whose portrait needs softening, before this reaches print? What would you change?
4. Would you be comfortable handing your essay to the person it describes and asking, "Is this fair"? If not, what does that discomfort tell you about the draft?
5. This collection will be a real, published book, read by your neighbors. How does knowing that changes what you choose to write, compared to writing only for yourself?

### Homework

Revise your Session 3 draft using today's guidelines: change any names or identifying details that could cause harm or legal exposure, reframe judgments as your own experience, and remove any private fact about another person that is not essential to your story. This revised draft is your first complete essay for the shared manuscript.

### Session 5 — The Arrival Essay: How and Why You Came to 2601

### Main Points of the Lesson

1. **Every resident has an arrival story, and arrival stories are natural essays.** They already have the shape we practiced in Session 2: a situation (life before 2601), a turn (the decision, the move, the first day), and an insight (what this building has come to mean since). The form does much of the work for you.
2. **"Why here?" is more interesting than "how here."** The logistics of a move (the broker, the paperwork, the moving truck) are usually summary material, covered quickly. The reasons — what you were leaving, what you hoped to find, what you were afraid of — are the scene-worthy heart of the essay.
3. **The first impression is a gift to a beginning writer.** Our senses are heightened on unfamiliar ground. What did the lobby look like the first time you walked in? What did your apartment smell like, empty, before your furniture arrived? First impressions are usually vivid in memory precisely because everything was new.
4. **An arrival essay can hold two time periods at once.** You can write the day you arrived and, in the same essay, reflect from where you stand now — years later, settled, changed. The gap between the person who arrived and the person writing today is itself a source of insight.
5. **Not every arrival was happy, and that is a legitimate essay too.** Some residents arrived after a loss, a divorce, a downsizing, a hardship. An honest account of arriving somewhere new during a difficult chapter of life is exactly the kind of true, resonant material this collection wants — handled, as always, with the care from Session 4.

### In-Class Practice

*Fifteen minutes.* Write the first paragraph of your arrival essay. Begin at a specific moment — walking through the lobby doors for the first time, signing the lease, unlocking your apartment door — rather than with broad background. Use as much sensory detail as you can recall.

### Discussion Questions

1. What were you leaving when you came to 2601? How much of that story belongs in this essay, and how much can be summarized in a sentence or two?
2. What is the single most vivid image you have from your first days in the building?
3. If you could tell your first-day self one thing about what 2601 would come to mean to you, what would it be?
4. Did anyone help you arrive — a doorman, a neighbor, a friend already living here? Is there a small scene involving that person worth including?
5. How is the "you" who arrived different from the "you" writing this essay today? Where does that difference show up most clearly?

### Homework

Complete a full draft of your arrival essay, 500 to 900 words, using the situation/turn/insight shape from Session 2 and the scene-building tools from Session 3. Apply the Session 4 safeguards to anyone else who appears in the story.

### Session 6 — The Neighbor Portrait: Someone Who Marked Your Time Here

### Main Points of the Lesson

1. **A great portrait essay is built from specific behavior, not adjectives.** Telling us someone was "kind" or "difficult" is a shortcut that skips the actual work. Showing us what they did — what they said in the elevator, how they kept their door, what they brought to the building barbecue every year — lets the reader form their own judgment, which lands with far more force.
2. **The best neighbor portraits are often about a small number of specific encounters, not a full biography.** You do not need to summarize twenty years of someone's life. Two or three sharply rendered scenes with that person will tell us more than a general character sketch ever could.
3. **A portrait can honor someone you have lost.** Many of the most moving essays about neighbors are, in effect, small memorials — a way of keeping a person present on the page after they have moved away or passed on. This is one of the most valuable services a building anthology can perform.
4. **Complexity is a compliment.** A portrait that allows a person to be difficult in one scene and generous in another is more truthful, more interesting, and — per Session 4 — considerably safer than a flattened, one-note portrait in either direction.
5. **Ask permission when you can.** If the neighbor you are writing about is still living in the building and the portrait is warm, consider simply telling them about the essay, or reading them a passage. Most people are moved, not offended, to learn a neighbor thought enough of them to write them down.

### In-Class Practice

*Fifteen minutes.* Choose one neighbor — past or present. Write three short, separate scenes (two to four sentences each) of specific things you have seen this person do or say. Do not use any adjectives of character. Let the actions speak.

### Discussion Questions

1. Which of your three scenes is the most vivid? What specific detail makes it work?
2. Is there a neighbor you have never properly thanked, and might this essay be a way of doing so?
3. If your subject is someone who has passed away or moved on, what do you most want a reader who never met them to understand?
4. Is there tension in your portrait — a way this person was hard to love, alongside the way you loved them anyway? How might including that tension make the portrait more honest?
5. Would you feel comfortable sharing a draft of this essay with its subject, if they are still living in the building? What might that conversation be like?

### Homework

Draft a complete neighbor portrait essay, 500 to 900 words, built from the specific scenes you generated in class. Apply Session 4's safeguards with particular care — this essay type carries the most direct exposure to another named, identifiable person.

### Session 7 — The Happy Memory: Joy, in Scene

### Main Points of the Lesson

1. **Happiness is surprisingly hard to write well.** Beginning writers often summarize joy ("it was a wonderful party" or "we had so much fun") rather than rendering the specific moment that produced it. Happiness, like every other emotion, is best delivered through scene and detail, not through the word "happy" itself.
2. **Look for the specific, not the general, celebration.** "The building always throws a nice holiday party" is a fact, not yet an essay. "The year the elevator broke during the rooftop party and twenty of us walked down eighteen flights of stairs singing carols" is a scene.
3. **Small joys are as valid as big ones.** A quiet Tuesday morning with a particular doorman's greeting, a recurring joke with the person who empties the recycling, the first time your building felt like home rather than an address — these small, undramatic happinesses often move readers more than a big, obvious celebration.
4. **Gratitude works better shown than announced.** Rather than writing "I am so grateful for this building," render the specific moment that produced the gratitude and let the feeling arrive in the reader without your having to name it.
5. **Happy essays still need a shape.** Situation, turn, insight still applies: what was true before this happy moment, what happened, and what you understand about your life at 2601 because of it. A happy memory without an insight can read as a list of nice things; the insight is what makes it an essay.

### In-Class Practice

*Fifteen minutes.* Write the specific scene of the happiest moment you can recall at 2601 — not a general description of a happy period, but one particular scene: a party, an afternoon, a small kindness, a celebration. Use as much sensory and physical detail as you can.

### Discussion Questions

1. What is the difference between telling a reader you were happy and showing them a scene that makes them feel it themselves?
2. Is your happiest 2601 memory a big, shared event, or a small, private one? What does that choice reveal about what actually matters to you?
3. Who else appears in this happy scene? What do you want the reader to understand about your relationship to them?
4. What insight does this happy memory give you about your life in this building — not just that it was a nice day, but what it means that it was a nice day?
5. Is there a form of happiness at 2601 — a particular kind of comfort, community, or peace — that is specific to living here, rather than something you might feel anywhere?

### Homework

Complete a full draft of your happy-memory essay, 500 to 900 words, ending with a clear insight rather than simply a description of a good time.

### Session 8 — The Hard Memory: Writing Loss, Conflict, and Difficulty With Care

### Main Points of the Lesson

1. **Difficult material belongs in this collection, and it can be written responsibly.** A building's true life includes illness, loss, conflict, fear, and grief, alongside its joys. An anthology that only contains happy material will read as thin and unconvincing; the hard essays, handled with care, are often the ones readers remember longest.
2. **Write toward understanding, not toward grievance.** The difference between a valuable hard essay and a harmful one is the quality of attention. We are trying to comprehend a difficult experience — what it did to us, what it taught us — not to settle a score or assign blame in print.
3. **You are always the safest subject in your own hardest stories.** Keep the focus on your own interior experience — what you felt, feared, misunderstood, or eventually came to accept — rather than asserting what others felt, intended, or deserved. This is both the most honest available material and, per Session 4, the most legally sound.
4. **Restraint often carries more weight than intensity.** A hard memory rendered with plain, exact language — stating what happened without embellishment or self-pity — usually lands harder on a reader than one told with heightened, dramatic language. Trust the facts of the scene.
5. **You control how much you disclose.** You may write about a hard experience obliquely, without naming every detail, and still produce a true, moving essay. Craft gives you tools — scene, image, implication — that let you be honest without being exposed beyond your comfort.

### In-Class Practice

*Fifteen minutes.* Write the opening paragraph of a hard memory from your time at 2601 — a loss, a conflict, a frightening event, a period of difficulty. Begin with a single, concrete image or moment rather than a general statement about how hard the period was.

### Discussion Questions

1. What is the difference between writing to understand an experience and writing to relitigate it? Which one are you doing in your draft?
2. Is there a way to tell this story that keeps the focus on your own experience rather than on judgments about anyone else involved? What would that revision look like?
3. What did this hard experience teach you about the building, your neighbors, or yourself — the insight that justifies including this material in a shared anthology?
4. Is there a detail you are considering leaving out because it feels too exposing? Would the essay still work without it? Sometimes restraint is itself the right craft choice.
5. Who, if anyone, should you speak with before this essay is included in the published book — and are you willing to have that conversation?

### Homework

Draft a complete hard-memory essay, 500 to 900 words, applying every safeguard from Session 4. If, after drafting it, you decide this particular story is not right for the shared, published anthology, that is a legitimate outcome of this exercise — not every true story needs to go in the book, and the practice of writing it is valuable regardless.

### Session 9 — The Funniest Experience at 2601: Writing Comedy That Is Also True

### Main Points of the Lesson

1. **Comedy is one of the most valuable tones a building anthology can hold.** A collection made up only of solemn material will not reflect the actual texture of life at 2601 — which, like any community, is full of the absurd, the awkward, and the genuinely hilarious. Funny essays belong in this book as much as any other kind.
2. **Specific, concrete detail is the engine of humor, exactly as it is the engine of every other essay in this course.** "Something funny happened with the movers" is not yet funny. "The movers got the couch stuck sideways in the stairwell for forty minutes while Mrs. Alvarez brought them lemonade and gave unsolicited advice through the door" is funny, because it is specific.
3. **Timing and restraint do more work than exaggeration.** The instinct of a beginning comic writer is to oversell the joke — extra adjectives, exclamation points, explaining why something was funny. The stronger instinct is to state what happened plainly and let the absurdity of the facts do the work. Trust the material.
4. **The best comic essays usually have someone at the center who is a good sport about it — often the writer.** Self-deprecating humor, where you are willing to be the fool in your own story, tends to land better and more safely than humor built entirely at someone else's expense. Review Session 4's guidance: making a neighbor the butt of a joke in print is a real risk, both to the friendship and legally, if it edges into an unflattering claim about their character rather than an account of a funny shared moment.
5. **A comic essay still needs the situation/turn/insight shape — the insight can simply be lighter.** The insight in a funny essay does not have to be profound; it can be as simple as "this is why I will never again lend my keys to anyone in this building" or "this is the story every new resident on our floor eventually hears." A light insight is still an insight, and it is what turns an anecdote into an essay.

### In-Class Practice

*Fifteen minutes.* Write the funniest true story you have from your time at 2601 — a mishap, a mix-up, an absurd coincidence, an overheard conversation, a moment that still makes you laugh when you think of it. Write it as plainly and specifically as you can. Resist the urge to explain why it's funny; just tell us exactly what happened, in order.

### Discussion Questions

1. Read your draft aloud to a partner or the group. Where did people laugh — and does that match where you expected them to laugh?
2. Is there a place in your draft where you're over-explaining the joke? What happens if you cut that sentence and trust the reader?
3. Are you the fool in this story, the straight man, or the observer? Does the story change if you shift that role?
4. Is anyone else in this story a real, identifiable neighbor? Would they laugh along with you if they read this, or would it embarrass or misrepresent them? Revisit Session 4 if needed.
5. Why does a building need its funny stories written down as much as its serious ones? What would be lost from this anthology without them?

### Homework

Complete a full draft of your funniest-experience essay, 500 to 900 words, ending on the lightest version of an insight — the small, wry truth this story left you with.

### Session 10 — The Most Unforgettable Experience & Workshop: Final Essay, Revision, and Building the Shared Manuscript

### Main Points of the Lesson

1. **Some experiences outrank all the others — the one you would tell first if someone asked, "What's it really like living there?"** This final essay assignment asks you to identify the single most unforgettable experience of your time at 2601, whatever category it falls into, and give it the fullest treatment of the course.
2. **By this session, you already have every tool you need.** Brainstorming found the material. Outlining gave it shape. Scene and voice made it vivid. The ethics session made it safe. This final essay is a chance to apply everything at once, on the strongest material you have.
3. **Revision is re-seeing, not just correcting.** Return to your strongest one or two drafts from this course with fresh eyes. Ask: does it start in the right place? Does the ending deliver the insight, or does it just stop? Is there a sentence you're keeping only because you worked hard on it, rather than because it earns its place?
4. **A shared manuscript needs a shape of its own, not just a stack of essays.** As a group, we will discuss ordering (chronological? by mood? alternating light and serious pieces?), a shared title, whether to include a foreword or introduction, and whether contributors want bylines, initials, or anonymity for any individual piece.
5. **Publication is a real, achievable next step.** A manuscript of this kind — ten to twenty short personal essays from residents of one building — is exactly the length and shape well suited to independent publication through a print-on-demand platform such as Amazon KDP, producing a real, physical book that can be sold or given to residents, at low or no cost to the group.

### In-Class Practice

*Twenty-five minutes.* Draft the opening scene of your most-unforgettable-experience essay — the single most vivid moment from the experience you have chosen. Then, using the remaining time, revise one earlier essay from this course by circling any sentence you are unsure belongs and asking why you kept it.

### Discussion Questions

1. Of everything on your original Session 1 brainstorm list, which item turned out to matter most once you actually wrote it? Were you right to guess it would be important, or did the writing surprise you?
2. As a group: should the shared manuscript be organized by theme (arrivals together, then portraits, then funny stories, and so on) or should it mix tones essay by essay? What effect does each choice create for a reader?
3. Is there a resident who should be invited to write a foreword — a longtime doorman, superintendent, or founding resident with a broader view of the building's history?
4. What should this book be called? Brainstorm titles together before deciding.
5. What is the plan — realistically — for turning this folder of essays into an actual published book? Who takes on which task: light copyediting, a simple cover design, the KDP upload itself, the printing order for residents who want a copy?

### Homework

Complete your most-unforgettable-experience essay, 600 to 1,200 words — the longest and most fully developed piece of the course. Submit your full folder of essays (as many as you completed) to the group for manuscript assembly.

## Glossary

**Brainstorming**
An unfiltered, quantity-first method of generating raw material for writing, in which the writer lists memories, images, or ideas without editing or judging them. Sorting and selection happen only after the list exists.

**Situation / Turn / Insight**
A simple three-part shape for a personal essay: the situation (what things were like before), the turn (what happened or changed), and the insight (what the writer now understands as a result). Nearly every short personal essay can be mapped onto this structure.

**Scene**
A moment of narrative rendered in close, sensory detail — as opposed to summary, which covers time and events more quickly and generally. Scene puts the reader inside a specific moment.

**Voice**
The distinct, recognizable personality that comes through a writer's specific word choices, rhythm, and way of noticing the world. In personal essay, voice should sound like an honest, particular person — not a generic "writerly" tone.

**Defamation**
A false statement of fact, presented as fact rather than opinion, that damages a real person's reputation. Personal essays that stick to the writer's own experience and clearly-framed opinions carry far less legal risk than essays that assert unverifiable claims about another person's character or actions.

**Invasion of Privacy / Public Disclosure of Private Facts**
A legal concern distinct from defamation: even a true statement about someone can expose a writer to liability if it discloses a highly private fact (medical, financial, or similarly sensitive) that the subject had a reasonable expectation would remain private, and the disclosure would be considered highly offensive to a reasonable person.

**Anthology**
A collected volume of writing by multiple authors, gathered around a shared theme. The finished product of this course is a resident-written anthology: a single published book containing every contributor's finished essays.

**Print-on-Demand (POD) Publishing**
A publishing model, of which Amazon KDP is the most widely used example, in which a book is printed one copy at a time as orders arrive, rather than in a large advance print run. This makes small, community-scale books like a building anthology financially practical to publish.

## Reading Companion Essay

### Why a Building Deserves a Book

Every apartment building accumulates a kind of oral history that almost never survives its residents. Someone remembers the year the boiler failed in January. Someone remembers the doorman who knew every child's name on the block. Someone remembers exactly how the light came through the lobby windows on the day they first walked in, hopeful and a little frightened, carrying a single box because the movers hadn't come yet. And then, eventually — because people move, because people age, because people pass away — all of it is gone, the way almost everything about almost everyone is eventually gone, unless someone writes it down.

This is not a small loss. A building like 2601 is not merely an address; it is a sustained, ongoing act of communal life — hundreds of small kindnesses, hundreds of small frictions, celebrations and losses experienced in close physical proximity to people who did not choose each other, exactly, but who ended up choosing, in whatever way, to stay. That is worth a book.

The personal essay is the right form for this project because it does not require its writers to have any particular credential or life story. It asks only for honesty and attention. You do not need to have done anything remarkable to write a remarkable essay about living here — you need only to look closely at what actually happened, choose the moments that matter, and render them so a stranger could feel them too.

What you are being asked to do in this course, session by session, is what every essayist has always done: to take the enormous, undifferentiated material of a lived life and select from it — deliberately, carefully, sometimes painfully — the handful of moments that carry the most truth. Brainstorming finds the material. Outlining gives it shape. Scene-writing makes it vivid. The session on writing about real people keeps it safe and fair. And the five essay types that follow — arrival, portrait, joy, hardship, comedy, and the most unforgettable experience of all — simply point that same set of tools at the different rooms of a life lived at 2601.

By the end, you will not just have learned to write personal essays. You will have helped build something that did not exist before: a permanent, physical record of what it meant to live, for however many years, in this particular building, among these particular people, at this particular moment. Long after any of us are still here, someone — a future resident, a grown child of a current one, a stranger who buys the book secondhand — will be able to open it and know, in specific and vivid detail, what it was like. That is not a small thing to have made. It is, in fact, a form of care for the people who will come after you, the truest kind a writer can offer: I was here. I paid attention. Here is what it was like.
