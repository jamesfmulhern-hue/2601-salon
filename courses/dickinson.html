Important Information & Course Materials▾
An Invitation▾

Emily Dickinson wrote nearly eighteen hundred poems. She published seven while she lived. She sewed the rest into small hand-stitched fascicles and left them in a drawer. When they emerged after her death, English poetry had to reorganize itself around them.

This four-session course reads Dickinson slowly. Six or seven poems per session — no more — and only the poems that reward the closest attention. Her dashes. Her capitalization. Her rhymes that almost rhyme but don’t. Her small stanzas that contain more universe than anyone else’s whole books.

This is a course for readers who have always been a little afraid of Dickinson, or who admire her from a distance and want to come closer. No prior reading of Dickinson is required. We move at the pace of the poems.

What This Course Is▾
  • A four-session close-reading course
  • Six to seven Dickinson poems per session
  • Ninety minutes per session, discussion-based
  • Non-graded, non-competitive, open to any adult reader
  • In person, adaptable to any partner venue’s calendar
What This Course Is Not▾
  • Not a biography of Dickinson (her life is fascinating but is not the subject)
  • Not a survey of nineteenth-century American poetry (Dickinson is her own century)
  • Not a poetry-writing workshop (though Dickinson’s craft lessons are inescapable)

What to Expect Each Week▾

Each session follows the same shape:

  1. Read aloud — every poem is read aloud by different readers before we discuss
  2. First response — what the poem gave you before you thought about it
  3. Close reading — line by line, stanza by stanza
  4. The Dickinson question — what did she do here that no one before her had done?
  5. Craft takeaway — one move a writer can carry away

A Few Promises▾
  • Every session begins on time and ends on time.
  • Every discussion is generous. Nothing said in the room leaves it.
  • No one is asked to speak. Silence is a legitimate contribution.
  • Dickinson is compressed on the page but generous in the room. We move slowly.
A Few Asks▾
  • Read the poems before the session. They are short; read each one twice.
  • Bring the printed or digital text. We refer to specific lines constantly.
  • Come to as many sessions as you can. Each session widens the last.

Session 1 — Dickinson's Small Rooms: Interior Landscapes

Focus: Six poems set inside a single consciousness. The mind at its smallest scale, seen with unforgiving clarity. Dickinson’s great subject: the room inside the room inside the head.

Main Points of the Lesson

  1. Dickinson’s compression is technique, not shortage. She said as much as she could fit in the smallest possible space — the opposite of Whitman.
  2. The dash is a Dickinson invention. It is not a comma, not a period, not a colon. It is a caesura and a hesitation and a leap all at once.
  3. Capitalization signals mid-line emphasis. Nouns capitalized are nouns Dickinson thinks the reader is looking at wrong.
  4. Slant rhyme is a Dickinson signature. She rhymes “fly” with “die” but also “soul” with “all.” The near-miss produces the emotional charge.
  5. The interior is her territory. No American poet before her — and few since — has mapped the small room of the mind so completely.

Reading

  • “I felt a Funeral, in my Brain” (280) — Poetry Foundation
  • “The Soul selects her own Society” (303) — Poetry Foundation
  • “There’s a certain Slant of light” (258) — Poetry Foundation
  • “Much Madness is divinest Sense” (435) — Poetry Foundation
  • “I dwell in Possibility” (657) — Poetry Foundation
  • “The Brain is wider than the Sky” (632) — Poetry Foundation

Critical Reception

  • Emily Dickinson — Poetry Foundation biographical entry — the standard scholarly starting point.

In-Class Practice

Take “I felt a Funeral, in my Brain.” Read it aloud three times, each time emphasizing a different word in the first line. What changes each time? (10 minutes.)

Discussion Questions

  1. What does the dash do at the end of a Dickinson line? Try reading a poem aloud replacing every dash with a period. What breaks?
  2. In “The Soul selects her own Society,” who or what is being shut out? Who is being kept in?
  3. “Much Madness is divinest Sense” — is this an argument, a lament, or a joke?
  4. Read “I dwell in Possibility” and “The Brain is wider than the Sky” back to back. What do they say together that neither says alone?
  5. Choose one poem from the six. Rewrite it without dashes and without capitals. What does Dickinson gain by breaking convention?

Homework

Read three more Dickinson poems on your own. Bring your favorite line to the next session.

Session 2 — Death Is Her Subject: Dickinson on Mortality

Focus: Nearly a third of Dickinson’s poems concern death, and no American poet has read the subject so calmly. This session takes six poems where Dickinson approaches death as an interlocutor — not a horror, not a comfort, but a companion.

Main Points of the Lesson

  1. Dickinson does not flinch. She looks directly at death. This is uncommon in the poetry of her century and rare in ours.
  2. She personifies death without sentimentalizing it. Death in Dickinson is a courteous carriage-driver, a fly, a house, a school.
  3. The plainness of her language conceals the depth of her thought. “Because I could not stop for Death” begins as innocently as a nursery rhyme.
  4. Every death poem is a life poem. Dickinson uses death to see life more clearly — that’s the point.
  5. Her afterlife is uncertain, and she wants it that way. Where a lesser poet resolves, Dickinson leaves the question open.

Reading

  • “Because I could not stop for Death” (712) — Poetry Foundation
  • “I heard a Fly buzz — when I died” (465) — Poetry Foundation
  • “After great pain, a formal feeling comes” (341) — Poetry Foundation
  • “Safe in their Alabaster Chambers” (216) — Poetry Foundation
  • “This World is not Conclusion” (501) — Poetry Foundation
  • “The Bustle in a House” (1078) — Poetry Foundation

In-Class Practice

Read “Because I could not stop for Death” aloud. Then read the last stanza only, aloud, three times. Where in that stanza does the poem’s whole meaning arrive? (10 minutes.)

Discussion Questions

  1. What is the tone of “Because I could not stop for Death”? Is Dickinson afraid? Is she comforted? Is she something else?
  2. “I heard a Fly buzz” ends with “and then / I could not see to see.” What is that repetition doing?
  3. In “This World is not Conclusion,” Dickinson considers belief and unbelief in the same breath. Which side does she come down on?
  4. The last stanza of “The Bustle in a House” is one of the most quoted moments in American poetry. Read it aloud. What makes it hit so hard?
  5. If you were teaching one of these poems to a person who does not read poetry, which would you choose? Why?

Homework

Bring one Dickinson death poem we have not read.

Session 3 — Nature and the Wild: Dickinson Outdoors

Focus: Dickinson lived largely at home, but her poetry moves through birds, bees, flowers, and storms with unmatched intimacy. This session reads six poems in which she goes outside — sometimes into her Amherst garden, sometimes into weather that has never been named quite so well.

Main Points of the Lesson

  1. Dickinson’s nature is close-up. No mountains. No sublime views. A single hummingbird. A single blade of grass.
  2. She resists the pastoral. Nature in Dickinson is unpredictable, sometimes menacing, often indifferent.
  3. The bird poems are theological. Dickinson does not use nature as decoration; she uses it as evidence.
  4. She names precisely. A bee is a bee. A snake is “a narrow Fellow in the Grass.” Precision is her spiritual practice.
  5. Nature in Dickinson does not console. It teaches — and its teaching is not always kind.

Reading

  • “A Bird, came down the Walk” (328) — Poetry Foundation
  • “A narrow Fellow in the Grass” (986) — Poetry Foundation
  • “‘Hope’ is the thing with feathers” (254) — Poetry Foundation
  • “There came a Wind like a Bugle” (1593) — Poetry Foundation
  • “I taste a liquor never brewed” (214) — Poetry Foundation
  • “To make a prairie” (1755) — Poetry Foundation

In-Class Practice

Take “A Bird, came down the Walk.” Read it as a piece of prose reportage, sentence by sentence. Then read it again as a poem. What did Dickinson gain by making it a poem? (10 minutes.)

Discussion Questions

  1. What is the mood of “A Bird, came down the Walk”? Where does it turn?
  2. Read “A narrow Fellow in the Grass” aloud. What is Dickinson doing with the word “Fellow”? Where else in the poem does language do double duty?
  3. “‘Hope’ is the thing with feathers” is one of her most-quoted poems. Is it also one of her strongest? Make the case.
  4. “To make a prairie” is four lines. Recite it. Then talk for ten minutes about it. What made ten minutes possible?
  5. Which of these poems most resists sentimentality? Which most invites it?

Homework

Look outside a window for five minutes. Then read “There came a Wind like a Bugle” again. What did you notice you had not noticed before?

Session 4 — The Great Late Poems: Dickinson in Command

Focus: By the 1870s Dickinson was writing poems that would define the reach of English poetry for the next hundred years. This session reads six of her most demanding, most rewarding poems — the ones she is still, more than a century later, teaching us how to read.

Main Points of the Lesson

  1. Late Dickinson is Dickinson at maximum compression. These poems are so compact they seem to bend the air around them.
  2. The lyric self becomes multiple. Late Dickinson speaks from angles no other poet has taken.
  3. Her subjects grow larger — and her language grows smaller. She wrote about eternity in words a child could pronounce.
  4. Reading Dickinson well means reading her more than once. The first reading is the appetizer. The third is the meal.
  5. She is not a nineteenth-century poet. By the end of her life she is writing a twentieth-century poetry that America would not catch up to until Modernism.

Reading

  • “Tell all the truth but tell it slant” (1129) — Poetry Foundation
  • “My life closed twice before its close” (1732) — Poetry Foundation
  • “Wild nights - Wild nights!” (249) — Poetry Foundation
  • “I cannot live with You” (640) — Poetry Foundation
  • “Pain — has an Element of Blank” (650) — Poetry Foundation
  • “Not knowing when the Dawn will come” (1619) — Poetry Foundation

In-Class Practice

Take “Tell all the truth but tell it slant.” Read it as a general reader would. Then read it as an ars poetica — a poem about how poems are made. What changes? (10 minutes.)

Discussion Questions

  1. Read “My life closed twice before its close” aloud. What are the “two closings”? What is the third that has not yet come?
  2. “Wild nights - Wild nights!” is either a love poem, a religious poem, or both. Make the case for each reading.
  3. “I cannot live with You” is one of Dickinson’s longest poems. Where does the argument turn? Where does it climax? Where does it collapse?
  4. “Pain — has an Element of Blank” reads pain as a temporal phenomenon. What is she saying about pain and time?
  5. Which of the six poems is the hardest? Which is the easiest? Are you sure?

Homework

None. Instead: pick a Dickinson poem we have not read together and read it three times. Bring it to any conversation you have this week.
